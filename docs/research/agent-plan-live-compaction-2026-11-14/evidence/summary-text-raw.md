## 3. Main usable research findings

### Keeper duties and records

- Keepers generally lit the lamp at sunset and extinguished it at sunrise.
- Wicks were trimmed roughly every **three or four hours**, depending on the station and period.
- Lens prisms, lantern glass, brasswork, chimneys, and rotating machinery were cleaned daily.
- Keepers maintained official journals recording weather, tide, barometric pressure, temperature, passing vessels, accidents, repairs, rescues, and unusual events.
- Monthly and quarterly reports were required for oil consumption, station condition, fog-signal use, absences, and related matters.

### Fog bells and striking machinery

- Early fog bells were often rung by hand; later mechanisms used falling weights, clockwork, governors, cams, hammers, and sometimes pulleys.
- Manufacturers included **Gamewell, Stevens, and Daboll**.
- Some mechanisms were advertised as capable of **10,000 strikes per winding**.
- Bell weights varied widely, commonly from roughly **600 to 2,000 pounds**, with examples up to 4,000 or 5,000 pounds.
- One official specification called for bell metal of **22 pounds tin to 78 pounds copper** and a bell weighing about 2,000 pounds.
- Historical signal patterns varied: examples include a double blow every 15 seconds, a single blow every 30 seconds, or ten strikes per minute. The author’s fixed rule of three strikes per minute takes precedence.
- Manual ringing after machinery failure is historically plausible and provides a useful rationale for Lin San grasping the rope.

Sources:
- https://uslhs.org/node/1722
- https://uslhs.org/node/1725

### Steam whistles, sirens, and acoustics

- Steam whistles operated under pressures often around **50–100 psi**; whistles ranged from small coastal examples to large locomotive-type whistles.
- The siren was generally considered the most powerful signal. Joseph Henry’s tests gave relative power ratings of approximately:
  - First-class siren: **9**
  - 12-inch locomotive whistle: **7**
  - First-class Daboll trumpet: **4**
- Relative fuel consumption was similarly about **9:3:1**.
- John Tyndall showed that sound transmission was highly variable. Fog itself did not always block sound, but wind, temperature layers, reflections, and “silent zones” could make signals disappear unexpectedly.
- Very long ranges of 20–30 miles were possible only under favorable conditions. Tyndall advised mariners to treat a heard signal as likely only **2–3 miles away** unless other evidence confirmed distance.
- The **diaphone** was mainly a post-1902/1903 development and would generally be anachronistic for an 1890s scene.

### Chinese Maritime Customs and China-coast lighthouses

- The Chinese Maritime Customs/Imperial Maritime Customs was formally a Chinese government service, though strongly influenced by foreign, especially British, personnel and practices.
- Robert Hart helped expand its Marine Department after 1868. Lighthouse construction and maintenance were funded substantially through tonnage dues.
- The service imported advanced European lenses and equipment and used David Marr Henderson as a major engineer.
- By 1892, one source records **104 lighthouses, 79 buoys, and 58 beacons** in the Customs system.
- **Gap Rock Lighthouse** was put into operation in 1892.
- **Waglan Lighthouse** was lit in May 1893, using a modern mineral-oil light and a rotating apparatus floating on mercury.
- Chinese personnel often served as native assistants, laborers, or lower-ranking lightkeepers; Chinese keepers-in-charge at major coastal stations were uncommon before the twentieth century.

### Supplies, families, and station life

- Lighthouse tenders delivered oil, coal, wood, food, mail, replacement parts, tools, and relief keepers.
- Landings at remote islands could be delayed for days or weeks by surf and weather.
- Married men were often preferred because their wives contributed to station operation, though the work was not always officially recognized.
- Children might be homeschooled, use correspondence courses, board on the mainland, or leave the station for school.
- Isolated stations combined bureaucracy, physical danger, monotony, and extreme dependence on scheduled supply ships.

### Station architecture and oil houses

- A station commonly included a tower, keeper’s dwelling, boathouse, fuel store, privy, cistern, and separate oil house.
- Dwellings could be attached to towers, linked by covered passages, or fully detached.
- Because kerosene was flammable, separate brick, stone, concrete, or iron oil houses became standard. They were intentionally set apart from living quarters.

### Typhoons and weather damage

- The October 1893 typhoon at Gap Rock broke several lantern-glass panels, drove rain into the service room, prevented the main light from being shown, and required temporary oil lamps. The light was restored by May 1894.
- Damage was intensified by the island’s exposed position, the shape of the seabed, and a gully that funneled waves upward toward the buildings.
- Waglan and other South China stations also experienced powerful storms, contaminated water supplies, damaged derricks, and sand-pitted lantern glass.

Source:
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710473/

### Chinese junks and navigation in fog

- Chinese sailors used magnetic compasses, sounding leads, star observations, seamarks, tidal knowledge, and seabed samples.
- Sounding leads weighing roughly 3–5 kilograms were coated with grease or soap to bring up sand, mud, or stone.
- Burning incense could also help estimate elapsed voyage time.
- Traditional navigation knowledge was often oral and supplemented by route manuscripts.
- Fishing and trading junks remained highly vulnerable when fog or typhoons arrived suddenly.

Source:
- https://apconf-much.org/proceedings/files/original/ca521fbf9871fd5ccea14958a5342eab.pdf

### Other atmospheric details

- Birds, especially during migration, flew into lantern glass and sometimes damaged lenses. Stations installed screens or wire guards.
- Tool chests contained saws, planes, chisels, files, braces, wrenches, hammers, and caulking equipment, supporting keeper repairs.
- Lantern glass had to be kept clear not only of soot but also frost, ice, snow, condensation, and salt spray.
- Folklore associated gulls and albatrosses with sailors’ souls and treated isolated lighthouses as uncanny or supernatural places. These are useful as beliefs or atmosphere, not as established facts of the plot.

### Coastal steamship routes

- Hong Kong was a major hub for coastal and regional shipping.
- Common routes linked Hong Kong with the China coast, Formosa/Taiwan, Macao, Indochina, Hainan, Japan, Singapore, and other Southeast Asian ports.
- Bulk cargoes included rice, coal, tea, sugar, beans, grain, raw cotton, iron, and kerosene.
